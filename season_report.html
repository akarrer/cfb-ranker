Two things I got wrong in the weekly reports

“The model's ratings are bunched together.” In the Week 5 report I compared the model's average predicted margin (9.3) with the average actual margin (17.7) and called it compression. That comparison is misleading: final scores always swing wider than any prediction, because games add noise on top. The right test is whether actual margins scale with predicted ones. Over 271 FBS games they do: actual ≈ 1.07 × predicted, versus 1.04 for the market. There's no bunching problem to fix.
“Stay away when the model disagrees with the market by 7+.” That rule came from the early cards, which used older versions of the model and the line at pick time. Replayed with today's model against closing lines, the big disagreements are no worse than the small ones — every edge size lands within noise of 50%:

What would actually make it better

  1. Better inputs, not more tuning. The structure is sound — margins are scaled right, results move teams by about the right amount, and probabilities are calibrated. The gap to the market is information: injuries, quarterback changes, and the PFF grades the model was designed for but has never had.
  2. Grade on closing lines from the start. The early cards' spread records were measured against lines from days before kickoff, which made them noisier and led to rules that didn't hold up.
  3. Lead with winners and probabilities. That's what it does well. Spread picks are break-even and should be presented that way.
